Elizabeth Crooke

Told by Elizabeth Crooke Lo contó Elizabeth Crooke

Sister of Jesus Crooke. Daughter of Mary Thelma Morel de Crooke (‘Mami’) and the late Charles Crooke. Hermana de Jesus Crooke. Hija de Mary Thelma Morel de Crooke (‘Mami’) y del fallecido Charles Crooke.

Elizabeth was born in Moca, in the Dominican Republic, in the neighborhood of Roque (?). She made a career in dance. The people who stood behind that career were her mother, her aunt Ana Luisa, and her uncle Manuel. Her father was a great dancer himself. He was not very involved at first. He accepted it later, without doing much to make it happen.

She said her nickname in an earlier take that did not survive, and she was not sure, while she was speaking, that the page was recording. What follows is the recording that did survive, in her words.

Elizabeth nació en Moca, República Dominicana, en el barrio Roque (?). Hizo carrera en la danza. Quienes la respaldaron fueron su madre, su tía Ana Luisa y su tío Manuel. Su padre era muy bailador. Al principio no estuvo muy metido. Después lo aceptó, aunque no se movió mucho para lograrlo.

Dijo su apodo en una toma anterior que no sobrevivió, y mientras hablaba no estaba segura de que la página estuviera grabando. Lo que sigue es la grabación que sí quedó, en sus palabras.

Crecí en un ambiente sintiéndome parte de una familia.

I grew up feeling part of a family.

The family she names La familia que ella nombra

Told by Elizabeth Crooke Lo contó Elizabeth Crooke

Crooke grandfather Abuelo Crooke

His personal name is not in the recording. Elizabeth describes English-speaking Black Caribbean people — “negros ingleses” — who settled (?) in the Caribbean. She thinks they were a textile (?) family, “or something like that.” Her grandfather was a chef in the Trujillo era. He was still living in the Dominican Republic when he met her grandmother. He could not adjust to the country, and he left. He left three children: her father Charles, her aunt Ana Luisa, and her uncle Georgie.

Su nombre de pila no está en la grabación. Elizabeth habla de negros ingleses que se radicaron (?) en el Caribe. Cree que eran familia textilera (?), «o algo así». El abuelo fue chef en la época de Trujillo. Seguía viviendo en la República Dominicana cuando conoció a su abuela. No se acostumbró al país y se fue. Dejó tres hijos: su papá Charles, su tía Ana Luisa y su tío Georgie.

Tavárez grandmother Abuela Tavárez

Her paternal grandmother’s first name is not recorded. Elizabeth says she was one of the Tavárez (?), and that the historic Tavárez Justo (?) family is part of that history. They lived around La México (?). Charles was born and raised in a barrio near there. Elizabeth had the name and then could not catch it while she was speaking.

El nombre de pila de la abuela paterna no quedó registrado. Elizabeth dice que era de los Tavárez (?), y que en esa historia están los Tavárez Justo (?). Vivían por La México (?). Charles nació y se crio en un barrio por ahí. Elizabeth tenía el nombre y, al hablar, no le salió.

Aunts, uncles, and a half-brother Tías, tíos y un medio hermano

Ana Luisa is Charles’s sister. Manuel, her husband, is an uncle by marriage. He has died. Elizabeth has lovely memories of him, and she names him with Mami and Ana Luisa as the people who supported her dancing.

Georgie is Charles’s brother, the third of the children the grandfather left behind.

Charlie, also called Cheche (?), is Elizabeth’s older half-brother on her father’s side. He has died. She does not name his other parent.

In Moca she had cousins, the daughters of Tía Cecilia, especially Yencenia (?), who was about her age. As they grew up she drifted away from those cousins, because their schooling and their upbringing pulled apart. The recording cuts off on that sentence. Tía Cecilia’s side of the family is not said. Tía Hilda’s side is not said either. Elizabeth hopes both Mami and Tía Hilda will talk about their own childhoods, “the people who came before us,” because you never really get to know everything.

Ana Luisa es hermana de Charles. Manuel, su esposo, es tío político. Ya no está. Elizabeth guarda lindos recuerdos de él, y lo nombra junto con Mami y Ana Luisa entre quienes apoyaron su danza.

Georgie es hermano de Charles, el tercero de los hijos que dejó el abuelo.

Charlie, a quien también llama Cheche (?), es el medio hermano mayor de Elizabeth por parte de su padre. Ya no está. Ella no nombra al otro padre o a la otra madre.

En Moca tenía primas, las hijas de tía Cecilia, sobre todo Yencenia (?), más o menos de su edad. Al crecer se fue alejando de esas primas, porque la educación y la formación se separaron. La grabación se corta en esa frase. No se dice de qué lado es tía Cecilia. Tampoco de qué lado es tía Hilda. Elizabeth quiere que Mami y tía Hilda hablen de su propia infancia, «las personas que nos anteceden», porque uno nunca llega a saberlo todo.

Someone she was said to resemble Alguien a quien decían que se parecía

The loved-ones recording opens mid-thought. Someone talked to her a lot about a woman people said she looked like. Elizabeth kept the photos and wondered whether the likeness was in the face or in the temperament. Who that woman was, and who was telling her, sat in the take that was lost. It is not guessed here.

La grabación de los seres queridos empieza a mitad de una idea. Alguien le hablaba mucho de una mujer a quien decían que Elizabeth se parecía. Ella guardó las fotos y se preguntaba si el parecido era el rostro o el temperamento. Quién era esa mujer, y quién se lo contaba, quedó en la toma que se perdió. Aquí no se adivina.

Childhood in Moca Infancia en Moca

Told by Elizabeth Crooke Lo contó Elizabeth Crooke

Her first memories are of the end of the street. She would lie on a coconut-palm frond, a yagua (?) palm sheath, a pod, or a plantain leaf, and roll down as if it were a slide (?).

She loved stones that were shaped so she could make a baby of them. She washed them, cleaned them off in the living room, and made a mess in the yard.

Decembers were kind. There were new clothes to wear for the first time at Christmas, and presents for Three Kings’ Day. She grew up believing in the Reyes Magos and leaving them a little something. She helped Mami build the twig tree Mami bought, with cotton and the angel hair — pelo de ángel — that made your skin itch like crazy. Her mother was the one who kept those family days moving.

Sus primeros recuerdos son el fondo de la calle. Se tiraba en una hoja de mata de coco, en una yagua (?), en una vaina o en una hoja de plátano, y rodaba como si fuera un tobogán (?).

Le encantaba jugar con piedras que tenían forma como para hacer un bebé. Las lavaba, les quitaba todo en la sala y hacía ese reguero en el patio.

Los diciembres eran agradables. Había ropa nueva para estrenar en Navidad, y regalos de Reyes. Creció creyendo en los Reyes Magos y poniéndoles su cosita. Ayudaba a Mami a hacer el árbol de palitos que ella compraba, con algodón y el pelo de ángel, que daba muchísima picazón en la piel. Su madre era quien movía esas actividades de la familia.

Me encantaba jugar con piedras.

I loved playing with stones.

Not yet asked Aún no preguntado

Left unmarked on purpose, with no dates invented to fill the gaps:

  • Her nickname, said in a take that did not survive.
  • The name of the barrio near La México where Charles was born.
  • Who the woman was that people said she resembled.

She asks that Mami and Tía Hilda be interviewed next. The interview is open for them, the same way it was for her.
