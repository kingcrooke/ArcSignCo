Interior Wayfinding | New York / Tri-State

Interior wayfinding and directory packages for New York buildouts

Interior wayfinding helps people choose a route and identify a destination through lobby directories, floor and elevator IDs, corridor decision points, room names, and accessible-route signs. Directional signs generally use visual information. Permanent room and space identification may require tactile characters and Grade 2 Braille. Arc works from plans, a survey, and a message schedule, then coordinates review, fabrication, and installation.

Scope

Package scope

An office wayfinding package is one commercial building signage system: people can find a tenant, a floor, a room, and the accessible route from the same message schedule.

Lobby directory signs

Building and tenant directories at the lobby, sized to the wall and to how the list will change after move-in.

Floor, elevator, and stair IDs

Elevator floor identification signs, stair IDs, and level markers at the points where someone decides to keep going or turn.

Corridor decision points

Interior directional signage and corridor signs where paths split, at elevator lobbies, and along the accessible route.

Room ID signs

Room names and numbers for offices, suites, and support spaces. Permanent room identification is planned apart from directional copy.

Restrooms and exits

Restroom identification and exit signs that the drawings call out, coordinated with the tactile package when those signs identify a permanent space.

Phased openings

Directory inserts, temporary directions, and later message updates when floors or tenants open in phases.

Navigation is not the same as room identification. Directional and informational signs generally use visual characters. Signs that identify a permanent room or space can require tactile characters and Grade 2 Braille. A directory is not automatically in that second group. Which signs need Braille is decided sign by sign, with the architect or code professional.

How the system is planned

Arc starts from decision points: the lobby, each elevator bank, corridor intersections, and the room itself. The sign schedule lists every message, location, and sign type so a multi-floor fit-out stays one package instead of a stack of one-off orders.

Finishes, insert systems, and mounting are shown on the shop drawings for architect and GC review before fabrication.

Room identification

ADA and tactile room IDs

Directional signs and tactile room identification signs follow different rules. Arc keeps them distinct on the schedule and does not treat a whole wayfinding set as tactile.

Under the 2010 ADA Standards, §216.2 covers signs that identify permanent rooms and spaces. §216.3 covers directional and informational signs through visual characters. Raised characters and contracted Grade 2 Braille, where they apply, are described in §§703.2 and 703.3. Mounting, including latch side and clear floor space, is §703.4.

NYC Building Code Chapter 11 adds identification of specified accessible elements and directional signs to those elements. BC 1111 is one of the provisions the project team should confirm for the occupancy. The architect or code professional decides which signs are in the tactile group.

Room, restroom, stair, and exit signs that need raised characters and Braille are fabricated with the ADA and tactile signs package, from the same message schedule. Arc prepares the drawings for architect and inspector review. Arc does not stamp drawings, and acceptance is the reviewer's call.

Plans and a survey come first. Then circulation and naming, a message schedule and shop drawings, architect and GC review, fabrication, and installation with punch-list coordination.

FAQ

Wayfinding questions

Do all interior wayfinding signs need Braille?

No. The 2010 ADA Standards cover permanent room and space identification in §216.2, and directional and informational signs in §216.3 through visual characters. A lobby directory or a corridor direction is not automatically a tactile sign. Source: 2010 ADA Standards, sections 216.2 and 216.3, and U.S. Access Board Chapter 7. Verify before publishing.

Which signs usually need tactile text and Grade 2 Braille?

Permanent room and space identification can require raised characters duplicated in contracted Grade 2 Braille. Directional copy is not automatically tactile or set in Braille. Building directories, occupant names, and company names and logos are treated differently from permanent room signs. Source: 2010 ADA Standards, sections 216.2 and 703.2 through 703.3. Verify before publishing.

Where should a tactile room sign be mounted?

Section 703.4 addresses latch-side location, mounting height, and clear floor space beyond the door swing. The project architect or code professional should confirm the condition at each door, including doors without latch-side wall space. Source: U.S. Access Board Chapter 7, section 703.4. Verify before publishing.

Does NYC have additional accessibility signage rules?

Yes. NYC Building Code Chapter 11 includes identification of specified accessible elements and directional signs to accessible elements, including BC 1111. Confirm the occupancy and the current code text with the project architect or code professional. Tactile room signs are covered on the ADA and tactile signs page. Source: NYC 2022 Construction Codes and the NYC Department of Buildings accessibility presentation, BC 1111. Verify before publishing.

Can Arc coordinate a multi-floor package?

Yes. Arc reviews plans, surveys conditions, prepares a message schedule and shop drawings, and coordinates fabrication and installation across floors, including phased openings. Arc does not file permits or stamp drawings.
