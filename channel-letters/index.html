Front-lit, halo-lit, and reverse channel letters for New York storefronts

Channel letters are individual three-dimensional letters, usually aluminum with acrylic faces, lit from inside by LEDs. Front-lit letters glow through the face. Halo-lit, or reverse, letters throw light onto the wall behind them, and some letters do both. In New York City, an illuminated storefront sign typically needs a DOB sign permit plus a separate electrical permit filed by a licensed electrician.

Styles

Channel letter styles

The right style depends on the facade, how the sign needs to read at night, and what the landlord allows.

Front-lit

Translucent acrylic faces with LEDs inside, so each letter face glows. A common choice when the sign needs to read clearly from across the street at night.

Halo-lit (reverse)

Solid metal faces with light shining out the back onto the wall, so the letters read as silhouettes with a glow behind them. The letters stand off the wall, and the wall finish affects how the glow looks.

Combination

Front-lit faces with a halo glow behind, for a layered look at night.

Non-lit dimensional letters

The same letter shapes without lighting, in metal or acrylic. With no electrical connection there is no electrical permit, but a sign permit is still usually needed unless the sign falls under DOB's exceptions.

Mounting

Mounting options

Direct (flush) mount

Letters are fastened to the facade one by one. It gives the cleanest look, but every letter needs its own attachment points and wiring path, so it depends on the wall, access behind it, and landlord approval.

Raceway

Letters mount on a narrow metal box that carries the wiring and power supplies. It means fewer wall penetrations and an easier removal later. The raceway stays visible and is usually painted to match the wall.

Backer panel

Letters mount to a panel that is then fixed to the facade. Useful on uneven or delicate surfaces, or where the building's sign criteria call for a panel.

How Arc picks: the facade material, sign band, access, and landlord criteria usually decide the mounting method. Arc reviews them at survey and shows the method on the shop drawings.

Approvals

Permits, power, and approvals in NYC

  • Sign permit. DOB says signs smaller than six square feet that are not illuminated do not need a permit, and it lists other exceptions in Construction Code §28-105.4.5. Illuminated channel letters generally need a sign (SG) permit, filed through DOB NOW: Build.
  • Electrical permit. A sign that needs an electrical connection needs a separate electrical permit, filed by a licensed electrician through DOB NOW: Build.
  • Electrical inspection. The NYC Electrical Code adds a local rule, §600.3(C): electric signs made for installation in the city are inspected and approved by DOB before installation. DOB can direct the inspection to happen at the factory before final assembly or at the install location.
  • Annual illuminated sign permit. If an illuminated sign extends beyond the building line, DOB says an annual illuminated sign permit may also be required. This comes up more with projecting and blade signs than with wall-mounted letters.
  • Zoning, landmarks, and landlords. The Zoning Resolution limits sign size, location, and lighting by zoning district. Landmarked buildings and historic districts need a Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) permit for signs and related lighting. Most leases and buildings also have their own sign criteria.

FAQ

Channel letter questions

What's the difference between front-lit, halo-lit, and reverse channel letters?

Front-lit letters have translucent faces, so the face of each letter glows. Halo-lit letters have solid faces and shine light out the back onto the wall, which creates a glow around each letter. “Reverse” and “halo-lit” usually mean the same style. Front-lit letters tend to read more directly at night, while halo-lit letters give a softer outline and depend on the wall finish behind them.

Do channel letters need a permit in NYC?

Generally, yes. The NYC Department of Buildings (DOB) says signs smaller than six square feet that are not illuminated do not need a permit, and it lists other exceptions in Construction Code §28-105.4.5. Illuminated letters generally need a sign (SG) permit plus a separate electrical permit. If an illuminated sign extends beyond the building line, an annual illuminated sign permit may also be required, which matters more for projecting signs than for wall-mounted letters.

Who handles the electrical for an illuminated sign?

A licensed electrician files the electrical permit through DOB NOW: Build and makes the connection. The NYC Electrical Code also requires electric signs made for installation in the city to be inspected and approved by DOB before installation (§600.3(C)). Arc coordinates the electrician and the power requirements. Arc is not an electrical contractor.

Who installs the letters?

Under Construction Code §28-415.1, a Licensed Sign Hanger must perform or directly supervise exterior sign installation, apart from the exemptions in §28-415.2, such as signs of 75 square feet or less that weigh 25 pounds or less. Arc coordinates a Licensed Sign Hanger where one is required.

Can my landlord or the building limit my sign?

Yes. Many leases and buildings set sign criteria for size, placement, materials, and lighting. The Zoning Resolution also limits sign size and lighting by zoning district, and a landmarked building or historic district needs a Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) permit, filed through Portico. Arc asks for the criteria at the start and designs to them.
