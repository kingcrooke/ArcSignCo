Permits

What needs a permit

DOB requires permits for sign displays and installations under Construction Code §28-105.1. On a typical storefront or building sign, that can mean:

  • A sign (SG) permit for the copy, meaning the text or image on the sign, filed through DOB NOW: Build.
  • An alteration permit for the structure, if the sign is attached to a structure instead of directly to the wall or facade.
  • An electrical permit for any sign that needs an electrical connection, filed by a licensed electrician.
  • An annual illuminated sign permit, which may also be required if the sign is illuminated and extends beyond the building line.

DOB lists the exceptions to the permit requirement in Construction Code §28-105.4.5. Signs that don't need a permit still have to comply with the Construction Codes, the Zoning Resolution, and the Rules of the City of New York, so zoning limits on size, location, and lighting apply either way.

Who decides: Arc reviews the likely permit path early and flags it at quote. The licensed applicant and the agencies make the final call on what the job needs and whether it is approved.

Submittals

Shop drawings, sign schedules, and submittals

Shop drawings show exactly what will be built and how it will be attached. For a sign package they typically include:

  • Elevations showing the sign on the facade or wall, with overall dimensions
  • Sign faces with letter heights, artwork, colors, and finishes
  • Materials and construction details
  • Attachment and mounting details for the wall or structure
  • Lighting and power notes for illuminated signs

A sign schedule, also called a message schedule, lists every sign in a package by type, location, and message. It keeps larger interior and ADA packages organized from submittal to install.

Depending on the job, the architect, owner, landlord, GC, and any licensed applicant review the drawings before fabrication. Clear, complete drawings make those reviews easier to run. They do not guarantee a timeline or an approval.

Landmarks and historic districts

If the building is an individual landmark or in a historic district, an LPC permit is needed before installing or replacing signs, awnings, or related lighting. LPC applications are filed through Portico, LPC's online permit portal. You can check a building's status on LPC's Discover NYC Landmarks map, and LPC's Permit Guidebook has separate chapters for signage and awnings that list what a complete application needs.

Landlord sign criteria

Many leases and buildings set their own sign rules, such as sign band size, allowed materials, lighting, and attachment methods. Arc asks for those criteria at quote and designs to them.

FDNY

Some projects also involve FDNY-related signage or review. Arc coordinates with the project team where that applies.

FAQ

Sign permit questions

Do I need a permit for a business sign in NYC?

Usually, yes. The NYC Department of Buildings (DOB) says signs smaller than six square feet that are not illuminated do not need a permit, and it lists the other exceptions in Construction Code §28-105.4.5. Signs that don't need a permit still have to meet the Construction Codes, the Zoning Resolution, and City rules. On a landmarked building or in a historic district, a Landmarks Preservation Commission (LPC) permit is also needed for signs and awnings.

Who can file a sign permit in NYC?

A registered design professional, meaning a New York State licensed professional engineer (PE) or registered architect (RA), can file. A Licensed Sign Hanger can also file for smaller wall-mounted accessory signs: up to 150 square feet and 300 pounds, projecting no more than 18 inches, and mounted entirely below the second-story ceiling (1 RCNY §105-04). Arc does not file as a licensed applicant. Instead, Arc prepares the drawings and information the applicant needs and coordinates with them, and with the project's expediter if there is one.

Who has to install an exterior sign?

Under Construction Code §28-415.1, a Licensed Sign Hanger must perform or directly supervise sign installation. §28-415.2 exempts some signs, including signs of 75 square feet or less that weigh 25 pounds or less, signs supported directly on the ground, directional signs, and temporary signs put up during construction and related to that work. Arc coordinates a Licensed Sign Hanger where one is required.

Does an illuminated sign need extra permits?

Yes. A sign that needs an electrical connection needs a separate electrical permit, filed by a licensed electrician through DOB NOW: Build. If the sign is illuminated and extends beyond the building line, DOB says an annual illuminated sign permit may also be required. Arc coordinates with the project's licensed electrician.

What if the building is landmarked or in a historic district?

You need an LPC permit before installing or replacing signs, awnings, or related lighting. Applications are filed through Portico, LPC's online permit portal, and LPC's Permit Guidebook lists what a complete signage or awning application needs. You can check a building on LPC's Discover NYC Landmarks map. Arc can prepare sign drawings and details to support the application.

What are sign shop drawings and a sign schedule?

Shop drawings show exactly what will be built and how it attaches: elevations, dimensions, materials, finishes, lighting, and mounting details. A sign schedule, also called a message schedule, lists every sign in a package by type, location, and message. The architect, owner, landlord, and any licensed applicant review them before fabrication. Complete drawings make review easier, but approval timing is up to the reviewers.
