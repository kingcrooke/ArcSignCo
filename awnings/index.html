Scope

New awning, recover, or replacement

A recover can keep a sound frame when projection and clearance still work. A full replacement fits when the frame, drop, or shape has to change. Layout starts from the opening and the survey, not a catalog nickname.

A recover is worth pricing when the frame is straight, the attachments are sound, and the projection, clearance, and profile can stay. New framing or a full replacement is the better scope when the frame is bent, rusted, or undersized, or when the drop, projection, or shape has to change. Send dimensions, straight-on and side photos, the projection, the clearance to the sidewalk, frame details, and the finishes you want to keep or change.

Traditional slope

A straight roof. Landmark staff criteria for a new fixed storefront awning generally call for this slope with open sides, an open underside, and lettering on an unframed skirt. Closed sides are a different review.

Quarter-round

A convex front on flat ends, built from curved bows. Graphics usually sit on the valance, where the face is easiest to read from the sidewalk.

Dome

A quarter-sphere over a door or a short window. An elongated dome stretches that curve for a longer front. Some shops call the long version a bullnose. The shop drawing names the profile.

Retractable

Fabric on a roller with lateral arms, so the cover can roll back. Useful where a fixed projection is not what the landlord or the landmark criteria allow.

Open sides show the frame and arms. Closed sides fill the ends with cover fabric and block more sun and rain. The shop drawing says which one, because it changes the frame, the fabric count, and how the awning meets the piers.

These side views are planning sketches, not fabrication details. Projection, drop, and valance height are set from the survey.

Graphics

Sign text and layout

Business names on the valance are the usual storefront identification, read from the sidewalk. Contrast, letter size, and sightlines matter as much as the wording.

Copy, a logo, or both can sit on a rigid valance or on a loose skirt. A light name on a dark valance, or the reverse, is the usual way to keep the words readable. The slope of the roof is a weak place for a long line of text, and at a landmark the guidance generally puts lettering on the skirt only.

In commercial and manufacturing districts, non-illuminated copy on an awning the Building Code already allows is the name or the address, in letters no taller than 12 inches and no more than 12 square feet. Residence districts allow less. The address decides which section applies.

Illuminated copy, or copy past the non-illuminated name-or-address allowance, is a sign. In most commercial districts, including C7, that sign may project only 12 inches, or 18 inches if it is double-faced. C6-5 and C6-7 allow up to 8 feet. Filing the sign does not raise the projection limit.

A tall front face, sometimes called a waterfall or a box, is one way to light graphics from inside. That face is a sign scope, with electrical coordination, not a plain fabric recover. When the facade should carry a separate illuminated identity, the storefront sign is channel letters.

Straight valance

A flat band. The most common edge, and the simplest place for a single line of lettering.

Scalloped

Repeated round lobes. The pattern should end evenly at both corners so the name stays centered.

Wave

A soft rise and fall. A low wave reads almost straight from across the street. A deeper wave is more decorative and leaves less room for letters.

Try the layout on the facade. Preview your awning in the Free Sign Preview: choose an awning shape, set scale, and place lettering on a storefront photo. It is a concept preview, not a shop drawing or a filing.

Approvals

DOB, DOT, landlord, and LPC coordination

An awning attached only to the building is a different project from a pole-supported sidewalk canopy. Landlord rules and landmark review sit on top of that split. Arc coordinates. The agencies decide.

  • Department of Buildings. DOB describes an awning or a replacement canopy under General Construction. DOB treats awning lettering as a separate or later Sign work type. Arc prepares drawings and information for the licensed applicant.
  • Building Code clearances. Provisions for a storefront awning over the sidewalk describe a projection of no more than 8 feet beyond the street line, frame clearance of at least 8 feet, a flexible valance that may come down to 7 feet, and an awning box or cover of no more than 12 inches. An awning over a window or door is described at no more than 5 feet. The awning has to be supported from the building to use those allowances. Confirm the current text for the address.
  • Department of Transportation. A cover on posts from the entrance to the curb is a sidewalk canopy. NYC311 puts that permit with DOT, not with the building-attached awning path. Width, height, length, and lettering rules are different. Confirm which structure the storefront actually is.
  • Landlord awning criteria. Leases often limit projection, color, copy, lighting, and how the frame attaches. Arc asks for the criteria before the layout is drawn.
  • Landmarks. On a designated property, LPC says awning installation or replacement needs an LPC permit, filed through Portico. Staff criteria for a new fixed storefront awning generally call for a slope, open sides, an open underside, a matte cover in a solid color or vertical stripes, and an unframed skirt no more than 12 inches with the lettering on that skirt. A retractable awning may be straight or curved. These are review criteria, not an approval.
  • Landlord and landmark coordination. Drawings for the licensed applicant. Arc does not file.

Installation is through our licensed partners. §28-415.2 lists cases that may be exempt; whether one applies is confirmed with the licensed applicant during survey. Filing details are on sign permits and shop drawings.

Fabrication and installation are through our licensed partners. Arc does not file permits or stamp drawings.

Fabrication

Materials and process

A finished awning is a frame, cover, valance, and drainage path that keeps water off the sidewalk. The survey checks utilities, clearance, and how the crew will reach the facade.

Frame

Aluminum or steel tube, welded or bolted, with rafters and arms spaced for the span. A rigid valance is part of the frame. A loose valance hangs below it. Commercial valances are often about 8 to 10 inches. At a landmark, staff criteria generally call for an unframed skirt no more than 12 inches. These are review criteria, not an approval.

Covers

Solution-dyed acrylic is the matte woven canvas. Vinyl-coated polyester is smoother and can be welded at the seams. Translucent vinyl is the cover when the face is lit from inside. Metal panels, standing seam or flat, are the unlit alternative. Vertical stripes run from the wall toward the front edge.

Drainage and access

The code calls for water from a rigid awning, canopy, or marquee to stay off the public walking surface. Flexible covers are treated differently. The drawing notes the pitch, any gap at the wall, and the sidewalk clearance under the frame and under the valance.

Cut sheets

Landmark work needs material specifications and manufacturer cut sheets. Arc names a fabric fire-retardant only when that product's documentation says so. A color name or a finish sample is not that documentation.

FAQ

Awning questions

How are awnings and awning lettering reviewed in NYC?

DOB NOW uses General Construction for an awning and for replacement of an existing canopy, and a Sign filing for lettering that needs a permit. A non-illuminated sign under 6 square feet can fall under the permit exception. Marquees are filed in BIS. A lit awning also needs the electrical permit, and an illuminated sign that extends past the building line needs the annual illuminated-sign permit. Arc prepares drawings for the licensed applicant and does not file as that applicant. Filing details are on the sign permits and shop drawings page.

What is the difference between an awning and a sidewalk canopy?

NYC311 describes a building-attached awning as under Department of Buildings jurisdiction. A pole-supported cover over the sidewalk is a canopy and needs a Department of Transportation permit. Confirm which one the storefront is before drawings go out.

Does lettering on an awning need its own filing?

DOB treats awning lettering as a separate or later Sign work type. One filing does not automatically cover the frame and the copy.

What if the storefront is landmarked or in a historic district?

The Landmarks Preservation Commission says awning installation or replacement on a designated property needs an LPC permit, filed through Portico. Check the building on the LPC map and the awnings guidance before work starts. Fixed awnings at landmarks are generally expected to have a straight slope and open sides, with an open underside and lettering on an unframed skirt.

Can you specify fire-retardant fabric?

Only when the product documentation for that fabric says so. The Landmarks Preservation Commission calls for material specifications and manufacturer cut sheets, and says awnings and canopies must meet the applicable Zoning Resolution, Department of Transportation, and Building Code requirements. Arc does not describe a fabric as fire-retardant without that documentation.

What should I send for a quote?

Send photos of the storefront, dimensions, frame details if an awning is already there, artwork and copy, landlord criteria, landmark status, the deadline, and the street address.
